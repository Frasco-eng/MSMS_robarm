\chapter{Thermo-fluid Systems}
\label{ch:thermofluid}
%══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Thermal and fluid systems require special treatment in the bond graph framework.
The fundamental difficulty is that heat (energy exchange due to temperature gradients) does not fit naturally into the standard power-product $e \cdot f$ = power unless the correct thermodynamic variables are chosen.
There are two approaches: the simple \textbf{pseudo-bond graph} (useful for heat conduction problems but limited) and the rigorous \textbf{true bond graph} based on entropy flow as the flow variable.

\section{Thermodynamic background}

\paragraph{Classical vs.\ irreversible thermodynamics.}
Classical thermodynamics studies equilibrium states and the energy accounting between them, without modelling the transition process.
\textbf{Irreversible thermodynamics} models the dynamic evolution — how a system transitions from one state to another via heat flows and work — under the assumption of \emph{local pseudo-equilibrium}: at any instant and for any small volume element, the system is in thermodynamic equilibrium, allowing the use of equilibrium state functions locally.

\paragraph{Internal energy and the Gibbs equation.}
The \textbf{internal energy} per unit mass $u$ of a pure substance depends on entropy per unit mass $s$ and specific volume $v = 1/\rho$:
\begin{equation}
  u = u(s, v).
\end{equation}
The fundamental thermodynamic relation (\textbf{Gibbs equation}) gives:
\begin{equation}
  du = T\,ds - p\,dv,
\end{equation}
where $T > 0$ is temperature (absolute) and $p > 0$ is thermodynamic pressure.
In rate form:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{u} = T\dot{s} - p\dot{v}.
\end{equation}
The equations of state are the partial derivatives:
\begin{equation}
  T = \frac{\partial u}{\partial s}\bigg|_v, \qquad p = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial v}\bigg|_s.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Entropy and disorder.}
Entropy $S$ quantifies microscopic disorder: the number of possible different microscopic arrangements the particles of a system can assume WITHOUT altering the macroscopic observable state (defined by pressure, temperature, volume). It is macroscopically quantified as $S = k_B \ln \Omega$, where $k_B$ is the Boltzmann constant and $\Omega$ is the number of microstates.
Heat flow between systems at different temperatures is always spontaneous from high-$T$ to low-$T$, always irreversible, and always increases total entropy (second law).
This irreversibility is the thermodynamic arrow of time. In fact, all physical laws potentially admit time to flow both forward and backward, but time actually always evolves in a forward direction because that is the direction mathematically related to an increase in global entropy.

\paragraph{The thermodynamic laws summary.}
Before proceeding, it is useful to recall the governing principles:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Zeroth Law:} Transitivity of thermal equilibria (if A is in eq. with B, and B with C, then A is in eq. with C). This defines temperature as a universal comparable quantity.
    \item \textbf{First Law:} Energy conservation, embodied in the Gibbs equation $\Delta U = Q - W$.
    \item \textbf{Second Law:} For any possible heat flow, the net and global entropy change is ALWAYS increasing. This defines the irreversibility of time.
    \item \textbf{Third Law:} As $T \to 0$ K, the system's entropy tends to stabilize to a minimum $S^{\min} \ge 0$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{insightbox}[Heat vs.\ work — the key distinction]
\textbf{Heat} $\dot{Q}$ is energy exchange driven by temperature gradients;
it changes internal energy via the entropy term $T\dot{S}$. \textbf{Work} $\dot{W}$ is energy exchange via macroscopic forces;
it changes internal energy via $-p\dot{V}$. The critical difference: work is ``ordered'' energy (zero entropy increase in an ideal process);
heat is ``disordered'' energy (always increases entropy). In nature, \emph{pure work exchange is never possible} — any macroscopic process generates some entropy increase (dissipation), which appears as heat.
\end{insightbox}

\section{Pseudo-bond graph for heat transfer}

The simplest approach: treat temperature as effort and heat flow rate $\dot{Q}$ as flow. Note that heat flow itself already has the dimensions of power.
\begin{equation}
  e \leftrightarrow T, \quad f \leftrightarrow \dot{Q}, \quad \text{but}\quad e\cdot f = T\dot{Q} \neq \text{power}.
\end{equation}
The product $T\dot{Q}$ has units of [K$\cdot$W] — it is \emph{not} power.
This is a \textbf{pseudo-bond graph}: it uses bond graph algebra but with non-power variables.
Despite this limitation, pseudo-bond graphs give the correct heat transfer equations:

\paragraph{Thermal resistor.}
Heat flows through a slab of material with thermal conductivity $k$, thickness $l$, area $A$:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{Q} = \frac{T_1 - T_2}{R}, \quad R = \frac{l}{kA}.
\end{equation}
In the pseudo-bond graph, this is an R-element with $R = l/(kA)$, connected to two 0-junctions representing the two surface temperatures.
The 1-junction implements $T_1 - T_2$.

\paragraph{The missing element: Thermal Inertia.}
It is crucial to note that \textbf{there are no thermal inertias}. Let us suppose a thermal I-element existed. In the pseudo-bond graph, its constitutive law would be $\Delta T = I \frac{d\dot{Q}}{dt}$. This implies that a heat flow $\dot{Q}$ could have a constant, non-zero value even if the temperature difference $\Delta T$ is zero, or it could flow against a temperature gradient due to a sort of "thermal momentum". This directly violates the Second Law of Thermodynamics, which postulates that spontaneous heat flow must always be directed from a higher to a lower temperature. Therefore, a thermal I-element cannot exist, as it would imply the possibility of violating fundamental thermodynamics.

\paragraph{Thermal capacitance.}
A lump of material with uniform temperature $T$, specific heat $c$, and mass $m$ absorbs heat:
\begin{equation}
  C\,\dot{T} = \dot{Q}_\mathrm{in} - \dot{Q}_\mathrm{out}, \qquad C = mc.
\end{equation}
This is a C-element in the pseudo-bond graph. Causality: $\dot{Q}$ is the flow (input), $T$ is the effort (output) — standard integral causality. The expression for $C$ can be formally derived by assuming the element does negligible work by expanding or contracting (constant volume). From the Gibbs equation $du = T\,ds - p\,dv$, if $dv = 0$, we have $du = T\,ds = dQ/m$. Since the specific heat at constant volume defines $du = c_v dT$, we obtain $dQ = m c_v dT$, or $\dot{Q} dt = m c_v dT$. Integrating this yields $T = T_0 + \frac{1}{mc_v}\int \dot{Q} dt$. Thus, the thermal capacitance is exactly $C = mc_v$.

\begin{warningbox}[Pseudo-bond graphs cannot couple to true bond graphs]
The pseudo-bond graph cannot be directly coupled to a true mechanical or electrical bond graph via power bonds, because $T\dot{Q} \neq $ power.
Coupling requires ad-hoc elements (e.g.\ a mechanical-thermal R-field) that handle the power conversion explicitly.
This limitation motivates the true bond graph approach.
\end{warningbox}

\section{True bond graph for thermodynamics}

The correct power variable pair for thermal systems is:
\begin{equation}
  e \leftrightarrow T \quad\text{(temperature, effort)},
  \qquad
  f \leftrightarrow \dot{S} \quad\text{(entropy flow rate, flow)},
  \qquad
  P = T\dot{S} = \dot{Q}.
\end{equation}
With this choice, $e\cdot f = T\dot{S}$ \emph{is} the true power (the heat power $\dot{Q}$), since $\dot{Q} = T\dot{S}$ for a reversible heat exchange.
This is the \textbf{true bond graph} for thermal systems.

\paragraph{The pure substance as a C-field.}
The Gibbs equation $\dot{u} = T\dot{s} - p\dot{v}$ reveals that a pure substance, viewed as a 2-port element, is a \textbf{C-field}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item Port 1 (thermal): effort $T$, flow $\dot{S}$, displacement $S$.
  \item Port 2 (mechanical): effort $p$, flow $-\dot{V}$, displacement $-V$ (or $\dot{V}$ with sign absorbed into orientation).
\end{itemize}
The constitutive law of the C-field is $T = T(S,V)$, $p = p(S,V)$ — the equations of state of the substance.
The energy stored in the C-field is the internal energy $U(S,V) = mu(s,v)$.

\section{Equations of state for a perfect gas}

For a perfect (ideal) gas, $pv = RT$ (where $R$ is the specific gas constant) and $u = c_v(T-T_0)$.
Using the Gibbs equation to find $u(s,v)$:

From $du = T\,ds - p\,dv$, with $p = RT/v$ and $u = c_v T$:
\begin{equation}
  ds = \frac{du}{T} + \frac{p}{T}dv = c_v\frac{dT}{T} + R\frac{dv}{v}
  \quad\Rightarrow\quad
  s = c_v\ln\frac{T}{T_0} + R\ln\frac{v}{v_0}.
\end{equation}
Inverting to express $T$ in terms of $s$ and $v$:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{T(s,v) = T_0\,\exp\!\left(\frac{s}{c_v}\right)\left(\frac{v}{v_0}\right)^{-R/c_v}}
  \qquad\Rightarrow\quad
  p(s,v) = -\frac{\partial u}{\partial v}\bigg|_s = \frac{RT}{v} = p_0\,\exp\!\left(\frac{s}{c_v}\right)\left(\frac{v}{v_0}\right)^{-\frac{c_p}{c_v}}.
\end{equation}
These are the \textbf{complete constitutive equations} for the perfect-gas C-field in integral causality (inputs: $\dot{S}$, $\dot{V}$; outputs: $T$, $p$).
The constitutive law is nonlinear even for a perfect gas.

\begin{insightbox}[Maxwell reciprocal relations for the gas C-field]
The Maxwell reciprocal condition $\partial T/\partial(-V)|_S = \partial(-p)/\partial S|_V$ is equivalent to the compatibility of the Gibbs equation — it holds by construction when the constitutive law is derived from a state function $u(s,v)$.
Always derive equations of state from a potential (internal energy, enthalpy, Helmholtz or Gibbs free energy) to guarantee this consistency. Arbitrary constitutive laws would not, in general, allow the existence of a conservative internal energy function, potentially allowing net energy production in a cycle (a perpetual motion machine of the first kind).
\end{insightbox}

\paragraph{Legendre transforms and mixed causality.}
When mixed causality is needed on the C-field (e.g.\ pressure $p$ as input, volume $V$ as output, while entropy $S$ remains input with temperature $T$ as output), the appropriate potential is the \textbf{enthalpy} $h(s,p) = u + pv$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial h}{\partial s} = T, \qquad \frac{\partial h}{\partial p} = v.
\end{equation}
The full set of Legendre transforms:
\begin{align}
  h(s,p) &= u + pv \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial h}{\partial s}=T,\;\frac{\partial h}{\partial p}=v \quad\text{(enthalpy)}\\
  f(T,v) &= u - Ts \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial f}{\partial T}=-s,\;\frac{\partial f}{\partial v}=-p \quad\text{(Helmholtz)}\\
  g(T,p) &= u + pv - Ts \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial g}{\partial T}=-s,\;\frac{\partial g}{\partial p}=v \quad\text{(Gibbs free energy)}
\end{align}
Each corresponds to a different causal form of the C-field.

\section{True bond graph for heat transfer: the thermal resistor}

In the true bond graph, a thermal resistor is a \textbf{2-port R-field}.
Two reservoirs at temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$ exchange heat $\dot{Q} = H(T_1 - T_2)$ through the resistor.
With $\dot{Q} = T_i\dot{S}_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{S}_1 = \frac{\dot{Q}}{T_1} = \frac{H(T_1-T_2)}{T_1}, \qquad
  \dot{S}_2 = \frac{\dot{Q}}{T_2} = \frac{H(T_1-T_2)}{T_2}.
\end{equation}
The net entropy production rate is:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{S}_2 - \dot{S}_1 = H(T_1-T_2)\left(\frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{T_1}\right) = H\frac{(T_1-T_2)^2}{T_1 T_2} \geq 0.
\end{equation}
This is always non-negative (second law of thermodynamics).
Unlike the pseudo-bond graph resistor, the true R-field is \emph{not} symmetric in entropy flow: more entropy leaves the hot side ($\dot{S}_1$) than enters the cold side ($\dot{S}_2$) — entropy is generated by the irreversible heat flow. Although the 2-port resistive field is power-conservative ($T_1\dot{S}_1 = T_2\dot{S}_2$), it is \textbf{neither a transformer nor a gyrator}, because it strictly increases the entropy of the system independently of the heat flow direction.

\begin{warningbox}[The only admissible causality for the thermal R-field]
The constitutive relation $\dot{S}_i = H(T_1-T_2)/T_i$ shows that \emph{both temperatures must be inputs} to compute the entropy flows.
This means both effort-in causalities are imposed: $T_1$ and $T_2$ are inputs, $\dot{S}_1$ and $\dot{S}_2$ are outputs.
No other causal form is admissible.

\section{Thermomechanical coupling}

A mechanically dissipative element (e.g.\ a friction force) dissipates power $\dot{W}_\mathrm{diss} = F_f \dot{x} > 0$.
In the true bond graph, this power heats the surrounding fluid or structure: $T\dot{S}_\mathrm{gen} = F_f\dot{x}$.
The dissipated mechanical power enters the thermal C-field via its TS port.
This is modelled as a \textbf{2-port R-field} connecting the mechanical port $(F, \dot{x})$ to the thermal port $(T, \dot{S})$.
The constitutive equations are:
\begin{equation}
  F_f = A\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot{x}) \quad\text{(Coulomb friction)},
  \qquad
  \dot{S}_\mathrm{gen} = \frac{|F_f\dot{x}|}{T} = \frac{A|\dot{x}|}{T} \geq 0.
\end{equation}
This models a situation where frictional energy reaches the fluid instantaneously (a standard simplifying assumption).

\section{Thermoelectric R-element}

A resistor dissipating electrical power at rate $ei$ also converts that power to heat, raising its temperature $T$.
The true bond graph couples the electrical port $(e, i)$ to the thermal port $(T, \dot{S})$:
\begin{equation}
  ei = T\dot{S} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot{S} = \frac{ei}{T}.
\end{equation}
If $R(T) = R_0(1 + \gamma(T - T_0))$ (temperature-dependent resistance), the constitutive law coupling all four variables is nonlinear.
This is the \textbf{thermoelectric R-field} — a 2-port element with one electrical port and one thermal port, physically representing the Joule heating effect.

If the resistor's body temperature $T$ is varying and depends on how much thermal energy is stored in it, it must be modeled as a thermal C-element attached to the 0-junction. Assuming no significant power flows at the $p\dot{V}$ port (negligible expansion work against the atmosphere), we use the linear relation $\Delta T = Q/C$. However, we must express $T$ as a function of $S$. Since $dQ = T dS$, we have $dT = \frac{T dS}{C}$, which integrates to $S = C \ln(T/T_0)$. Inverting this yields the nonlinear constitutive equation for the resistor's thermal capacitance in the true bond graph:
\begin{equation}
  T(S) = T_0 e^{S/C}.
\end{equation}

\begin{insightbox}[True vs.\ pseudo in practice]
The true bond graph (with $T, \dot{S}$ as thermal port variables) is conceptually correct and allows seamless coupling to mechanical and electrical domains via standard power bonds.
The pseudo-bond graph ($T, \dot{Q}$) is simpler and sufficient for pure heat conduction problems with no coupling to other domains.
For mixed thermomechanical or thermoelectric systems — which is the case for most real mechatronic components — the true formulation is necessary.
\end{insightbox}

\section{Fluid dynamic systems revisited: Eulerian approach and Bernoulli}

Previously, fluid systems were modeled using a simple 1D lumped parameter approach (Lagrangian perspective). While plausible for short segments and linear elements, estimating the fluid inertia $I = \rho L / A$ has severe limitations for transient conditions, varying density, or varying cross-sectional areas. 

A more accurate description requires the \textbf{Eulerian approach}, where observations are made at a fixed point in space as different fluid particles pass by. The Eulerian Newton's law relates the velocity field to the pressure gradient and gravity:
\begin{equation}
  \rho\frac{Dv}{Dt} = \rho\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + v\frac{\partial v}{\partial s}\right) = -\frac{\partial p}{\partial s} - \rho g\frac{dz}{ds}
\end{equation}
where $D/Dt$ is the material derivative. For an incompressible fluid, $v(s,t) = Q(t)/A(s)$. Substituting this into the Eulerian Newton's law and integrating along the streamline from $s_0$ to $s_l$ yields the \textbf{Bernoulli equation} for the line element:
\begin{equation}
  I\dot{Q} + \frac{\rho Q^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{A_l^2} - \frac{1}{A_0^2}\right) = p_0(t) - p_l(t) - \rho g(z_l - z_0)
\end{equation}
where $I = \rho \int_0^l \frac{ds}{A(s)}$ is the generalized fluid inertia.

\paragraph{The Bernoulli Resistor ($R_B$).}
The term involving $Q^2$ represents the dynamic pressure associated with the kinetic energy of the fluid. In a bond graph, it is represented as an unusual resistance, the \textbf{Bernoulli resistance} $R_B$. 
\begin{equation}
  \Delta p_\mathrm{dyn} = \frac{\rho Q^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{A_l^2} - \frac{1}{A_0^2}\right)
\end{equation}
Unlike standard resistors, $R_B$ does not always dissipate power. It models the conversion between static pressure energy and kinetic energy (dynamic pressure). 
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
    \item If $A_0 > A_l$ (a nozzle), the fluid accelerates, kinetic energy increases, and static pressure drops. This operates in the \textbf{static power loss} region.
    \item If $A_0 < A_l$ (a diffuser), the fluid decelerates, kinetic energy decreases, and static pressure is recovered. This operates in the \textbf{static power recovery} region, where the resistance appears to \emph{supply} static power.
\end{itemize}

\section{Pseudo-bond graphs for compressible gases}

When dealing with compressible gases where density changes drastically, maintaining a true bond graph representation becomes computationally complex because the Eulerian description entangles inertial and compliance aspects. This motivates the \textbf{thermodynamic accumulator}, a specialized pseudo-bond graph element.

The control volume transports mass, so the flow variables become mass flow rate $\dot{m}$ and enthalpy flow rate $\dot{E}_h$. The accumulator acts as a multi-port C-field with integral causality, storing internal energy $E$ and mass $m$:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{E} = \sum \dot{E}_{h,\mathrm{in}} - \sum \dot{E}_{h,\mathrm{out}} - p\dot{V}, \qquad \dot{m} = \sum \dot{m}_\mathrm{in} - \sum \dot{m}_\mathrm{out}
\end{equation}
This formulation smoothly interfaces with restrictors (modeling isentropic nozzles) to build complete, low-order, accurate models of interacting thermo-fluid systems, such as piston-ported engines or air cylinders with leakage.
