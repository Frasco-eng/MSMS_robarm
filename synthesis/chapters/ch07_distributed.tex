\chapter{Models of Distributed Parameter Systems}
\label{ch:distributed}
%══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

All models considered so far are \textbf{lumped-parameter models}: the state is a
finite-dimensional vector, components are point-like, and the number of degrees of
freedom is finite. Real structures, however, have \textbf{continuously distributed}
inertia, compliance, and damping --- a vibrating rod, a flexible beam, a hydraulic
line, a heat-conducting bar. These are described by \textbf{partial differential
equations} (PDEs), and the number of degrees of freedom is in principle infinite.
This chapter develops two strategies for obtaining tractable finite-dimensional
bond-graph models: \emph{simple lumping} and \emph{separation of variables (normal
modes)}.

%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Distributed-parameter systems}
\label{sec:distrib_intro}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A \textbf{distributed-parameter system} is represented by a PDE: it cannot be
accurately approximated by a lumped-parameter model without significant loss of
information. The overarching philosophy is the same that governs all engineering
modelling: use the \emph{simplest model} that captures the behaviour of interest,
but resist oversimplification. In the memorable words often attributed to
Einstein, \emph{``Everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler.''}

So far it has been assumed that systems are built from components exhibiting
inertia-like, compliance-like, or resistance-like effects in isolated, point-like
fashion: a mass is perfectly rigid (no finite stiffness), and a spring is
perfectly compliant (no mass). Of course, all real materials simultaneously
exhibit inertial and compliance effects; it is our modelling \emph{assumptions}
that justify separating these effects into specific lumps. When those assumptions
break down --- for example when excitation frequencies become comparable to the
first natural frequency of the distributed body --- a distributed-parameter
description is unavoidable.

\section{Simple lumping techniques}
\label{sec:simple_lumping}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Derivation of the wave equation for a bar}
\label{ssec:wave_eq}

The simplest and most physically transparent route to a PDE model is to start
with \textbf{spatially distributed finite lumps} and then take the limit as the
lump size becomes infinitesimal.  We illustrate this approach with the canonical
example: the \textbf{longitudinal vibrations of a uniform elastic bar}.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/chapter7/7_1a.png}
  \caption{Elastic bar of length $L$, cross-sectional area $A$, and density $\rho$,
  clamped at $x=0$ and subjected to an axial force $F(t)$}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figs/chapter7/7_1b.png}
  \caption{Image of a single Lump}
\end{figure}

Consider a small element of the bar located between $x$ and $x + \Delta x$.
Applying \textbf{Newton's second law} to this element gives:
\begin{equation}
  A\bigl[\sigma(x+\Delta x) - \sigma(x)\bigr]
  = \rho A\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial^{2}\xi}{\partial t^{2}}(x),
  \label{eq:newton_element}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(x)$ is the axial stress and the right-hand side is the inertia
force of the element.  The \textbf{definition of linear strain} and Hooke's law
connect the stress to the displacement gradient:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma(x)
  = E\,\frac{\xi(x) - \xi(x - \Delta x)}{\Delta x},
  \label{eq:strain_def}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is Young's modulus.  Substituting \eqref{eq:strain_def} into
\eqref{eq:newton_element} and \textbf{taking the limit $\Delta x \to 0$} one
obtains the \textbf{wave equation}:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
    E\,\frac{\partial^{2}\xi}{\partial x^{2}}
    = \rho\,\frac{\partial^{2}\xi}{\partial t^{2}}
  }.
  \label{eq:wave_eq}
\end{equation}

Equivalently, defining the stress as $\sigma = E\,\partial\xi/\partial x$, the
two-field form reads:
\begin{equation}
  A\,\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial x}
  = \rho A\,\frac{\partial^{2}\xi}{\partial t^{2}},
  \qquad
  \sigma = E\,\frac{\partial\xi}{\partial x}.
\end{equation}


\subsection{The lumped approximation and its bond graph}
\label{ssec:lumped_bar}

Instead of taking the limit, one can \textbf{retain finite lumps} and construct
a discrete approximation.  Dividing the bar into $n$ elements of length
$\Delta x = L/n$, the state variables for the $i$-th lump are:
\begin{equation}
  p_{i} = \rho A\,\Delta x\;\dot{\xi}_{i}
  \quad\text{(momentum)},
  \qquad
  q_{i} = \xi_{i} - \xi_{i-1}
  \quad\text{(relative elongation)}.
\end{equation}
The discrete Newton and Hooke laws are:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}p_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}
  = \frac{EA}{\Delta x}\bigl(q_{i+1} - q_{i}\bigr),
  \qquad
  \frac{\mathrm{d}q_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}
  = \frac{1}{\rho A\,\Delta x}\bigl(p_{i} - p_{i-1}\bigr),
\end{equation}
with the auxiliary relations:
\begin{equation}
  F_{i} = \frac{EA}{\Delta x}\,q_{i},
  \qquad
  A\sigma = F,
  \qquad
  p_{i} = \rho A\,\Delta x\;\dot{\xi}_{i}.
\end{equation}


\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figs/chapter7/7_2.png}
  \caption{bond graph for the $n$-lump bar}
\end{figure}

In bond-graph language each I-element represents the momentum of lump $i$ and
each C-element represents its elastic compliance.  The 1-junctions impose
Newton's law (common velocity = $\dot{\xi}_{i}$), while the 0-junctions
impose Hooke's law (common force = $F_{i}$).


\subsection{Boundary elements and admissible configurations}
\label{ssec:boundary}

The first element ($i=1$) and the $n$-th element require special treatment since
they are \textbf{boundary elements}: they are not surrounded by lumps on both
sides.  The modified boundary equations are:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}q_{1}}{\mathrm{d}t}
  = \frac{p_{1}}{\rho A\,\Delta x},
  \qquad
  \frac{\mathrm{d}p_{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}
  = F(t) - \frac{EA}{\Delta x}\,q_{n}.
\end{equation}

To use this approach one first selects the \textbf{number of lumps} $n$; this
choice then sets the I and C parameters.  The number of lumps should be chosen
based on accuracy criteria, but it is generally not possible to determine \emph{a
priori} the rate of convergence to the continuous model, especially when the
discrete model is embedded in a larger system.

\subsection{Summary and limitations of simple lumping}
\label{ssec:lumping_summary}

Taking the limit $n \to \infty$ in the discrete model recovers the wave
equation~\eqref{eq:wave_eq}.  External forcing or active boundary conditions
can be incorporated in several ways.  For a point force $F(t)$ at $x = L$,
the PDE reads:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{A}\,F(t)\,\delta(x - L)
  + E\,\frac{\partial^{2}\xi}{\partial x^{2}}
  = \rho\,\frac{\partial^{2}\xi}{\partial t^{2}},
  \label{eq:wave_forced}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(\cdot)$ is the Dirac delta distribution representing a
\textbf{distributed force per unit length} concentrated at the boundary.

Two representations are therefore available for the bar:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Finite-lump representation.}  Physically transparent and easily
    embedded in an overall system bond graph.  Non-linear effects pose no
    particular problems.  However, the state space grows very quickly with $n$
    while the accuracy does not: each additional lump adds a new, very inaccurate
    high-frequency mode while improving the existing low-frequency predictions
    only mildly.  The resulting large disparity in time constants can create
    severe stiffness problems for numerical integration.
  \item \textbf{(Linear) PDE representation.}  Very accurate; admits analytical
    solutions.  However, it is difficult to incorporate into an overall system
    model, because non-linear effects introduce serious complications and coupling
    the PDE to the remainder of the system via boundary conditions is
    non-trivial.
\end{itemize}

\begin{insightbox}[The way forward: normal modes]
  The PDE representation can be manipulated using
  \emph{normal mode techniques} to yield an \textbf{accurate and
  computationally efficient finite-order model}.  This is the subject of the
  rest of this chapter.  The key idea is that the continuous bar can oscillate
  in an infinite set of orthogonal \emph{mode shapes}, and any motion ---
  forced or unforced --- can be expressed as a linear combination of those
  shapes.  Truncating the series at $N$ modes gives an exact model up to the
  $N$-th natural frequency.
\end{insightbox}

\begin{warningbox}[Use simple lumping with caution]
  The simple lumping approach \emph{must be used cautiously} and with awareness
  on the part of the modeller.  Even for seemingly low-frequency excitations a
  large number of lumps may be required, and convergence is hard to predict a
  priori.  Moreover, the high-frequency modes introduced by each additional lump
  are highly inaccurate and may corrupt both the numerical integration and the
  interaction with the rest of the system.
\end{warningbox}

\section{Lumped models via separation of variables}
\label{sec:modal}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Motivation and overview}
\label{ssec:modal_intro}

Many physical elements possess a \textbf{continuous distribution of inertia and
compliance} properties.  When linearity can be assumed, these distributed elements
can be represented by linear PDEs in space and time.  The actual dynamics of these
distributed systems are composed of \textbf{propagating waves} that reflect from
boundaries and interfere to produce the observed response at any point and time.
This wave-superposition property can be formalised and exploited for modelling
purposes via the \textbf{separation of variables} technique.

The outcome of this procedure is a \textbf{lumped-parameter bond-graph
representation} of the distributed element which can be seamlessly coupled to
bond-graph models of the rest of the system.  Crucially:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item The method works for \emph{general linear PDEs}, not only for wave-like
    systems.
  \item It provides a spectrally exact representation up to the retained number
    of modes.
  \item The resulting bond graph has all storage elements in integral causality,
    enabling efficient numerical simulation.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Separation of variables: the unforced bar}
\label{ssec:sov_unforced}

Consider again the longitudinal vibration of a bar governed by
equation~\eqref{eq:wave_forced}.  The method of separation of variables assumes
first that the external force is zero, $F(t) = 0$, and that the displacement
can be written as a \textbf{product of a purely spatial and a purely temporal
function}:
\begin{equation}
  \xi(x,t) = Y(x)\,f(t).
  \label{eq:sep_ansatz}
\end{equation}
This is the central (and daring) ansatz: it asserts that space and time
dependences factorise.  Substituting \eqref{eq:sep_ansatz} into the unforced
wave equation gives:
\begin{equation}
  \rho\,Y(x)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}f}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}
  - E\,f(t)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}Y}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}} = 0.
\end{equation}
Dividing through by $Y(x)f(t)$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{1}{f}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}f}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}
  = \frac{E}{\rho}\,\frac{1}{Y}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}Y}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}
  = -\omega^{2},
  \qquad \omega \in \mathbb{C}.
  \label{eq:sov_separation}
\end{equation}
The left side depends only on $t$ and the right side depends only on $x$; hence
both must equal the \textbf{same constant}, $-\omega^{2}$.  This yields
\textbf{two decoupled ODEs}:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}f}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}} + \omega^{2}\,f = 0,
  \label{eq:temporal_ode}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}Y}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}} + \frac{\rho}{E}\,\omega^{2}\,Y = 0,
  \label{eq:spatial_ode}
\end{equation}
which must be solved once proper boundary and initial conditions are provided.
The temporal ODE~\eqref{eq:temporal_ode} is a simple harmonic oscillator: once
$\omega$ is known, $f(t) = C_{1}\cos\omega t + C_{2}\sin\omega t$.  The
spatial ODE~\eqref{eq:spatial_ode} determines the \emph{allowable} values of
$\omega$.


\subsection{Eigenvalue problem and mode shapes}
\label{ssec:eigenproblem}

The general solution of the spatial ODE~\eqref{eq:spatial_ode} is:
\begin{equation}
  Y(x) = A\cos kx + B\sin kx,
  \qquad k^{2} = \frac{\rho}{E}\,\omega^{2},
  \label{eq:spatial_solution}
\end{equation}
where the constants $A$ and $B$ and the wave number $k$ depend on the boundary
conditions.  For the bar \textbf{clamped at $x = 0$ and free at $x = L$}:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item At $x=0$ the displacement vanishes:
    $\xi(0,t) = Y(0)\,f(t) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; Y(0) = 0$.
  \item At $x=L$ the stress $A\sigma(L,t) = EA\,\partial\xi/\partial x\,(L,t) = F(t)$
    balances the applied force.  In the \emph{unforced} case, $F(t)=0$, this
    reduces to:
    $\partial\xi/\partial x\,(L,t) = \mathrm{d}Y/\mathrm{d}x\,(L)\,f(t) = 0
    \;\Rightarrow\; Y'(L) = 0$.
\end{itemize}

Applying $Y(0)=0$ to \eqref{eq:spatial_solution} gives $A=0$, so
$Y(x) = B\sin kx$.  Applying $Y'(L) = 0$:
\begin{equation}
  k\,B\cos kL = 0.
\end{equation}
The non-trivial solution ($B \neq 0$) requires:
\begin{equation}
  \cos kL = 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  k_{n}L = (2n-1)\,\frac{\pi}{2},
  \qquad n = 1,2,3,\ldots
  \label{eq:freq_eq}
\end{equation}
This is the \textbf{frequency equation}, entirely analogous to the characteristic
equation for the eigenvalues of a lumped system.  The corresponding
\textbf{natural frequencies} are:
\begin{equation}
  \omega_{n}^{2} = \frac{E}{\rho}\,k_{n}^{2}
  = \frac{E}{\rho}\,\frac{(k_{n}L)^{2}}{L^{2}}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \omega_{n} = \sqrt{\frac{E}{\rho}}\,\frac{2n-1}{L}\,\frac{\pi}{2},
  \qquad n = 1,2,3,\ldots
  \label{eq:natural_freq}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding \textbf{eigenfunctions} (or \textbf{mode shapes}), with
normalisation $B_{n}=1$, are:
\begin{equation}
  Y_{n}(x) = \sin\!\left(k_{n}L\,\frac{x}{L}\right)
  = \sin\!\left((2n-1)\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{x}{L}\right),
  \qquad n = 1,2,3,\ldots
  \label{eq:mode_shapes}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{figs/chapter7/7_3.png}
  \caption{ First three mode shapes $Y_{1}(x)$, $Y_{2}(x)$, $Y_{3}(x)$}
\end{figure}

If the bar is displaced in the shape of mode $n$ and released, it oscillates
purely in that \textbf{modal configuration} at the corresponding modal
frequency $\omega_{n}$.  Any motion of the bar, from \emph{any} initial
conditions, is a \textbf{linear combination} of the mode shapes oscillating at
their respective natural frequencies.


\subsection{Orthogonality of mode shapes}
\label{ssec:orthogonality}

The mode shapes are \textbf{orthogonal} with respect to the mass distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{0}^{L} Y_{n}(x)\,Y_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0,
  \qquad n \neq m.
\end{equation}
For the specific shapes~\eqref{eq:mode_shapes} this evaluates to:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{0}^{L}
    \sin\!\left((2n-1)\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{x}{L}\right)
    \sin\!\left((2m-1)\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{x}{L}\right)
  \mathrm{d}x
  =
  \begin{cases}
    0, & n \neq m, \\[4pt]
    L/2, & n = m.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:orthogonality}
\end{equation}
This orthogonality property is the cornerstone of modal analysis: it will
allow us to \emph{decouple} the modal equations when the forced case is
treated.


\subsection{Forced response and modal decomposition}
\label{ssec:forced_modal}

We are ultimately interested in the \textbf{forced response} of the bar.  We
now exploit the fact that the response of the bar, whether forced or unforced,
can \emph{always} be expressed as a linear combination of the mode shapes.
Starting from the forced PDE~\eqref{eq:wave_forced}, we write:
\begin{equation}
  \xi(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} Y_{n}(x)\,\eta_{n}(t),
  \label{eq:modal_expansion}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_{n}(t)$ are the \textbf{modal coordinates} (time-dependent
amplitudes to be determined).  Substituting~\eqref{eq:modal_expansion} into
the forced PDE:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{n} \rho A\,Y_{n}\,\ddot{\eta}_{n}
  - \sum_{n} AE\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}Y_{n}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}\,\eta_{n}
  = F(t)\,\delta(x - L).
\end{equation}
Using the fact that the mode shapes satisfy $\mathrm{d}^{2}Y_{n}/\mathrm{d}x^{2}
= -(\rho/E)\,\omega_{n}^{2}\,Y_{n}$, multiplying both sides by $Y_{m}(x)$ and
integrating over $[0,L]$:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{n}
    \left(\int_{0}^{L} \rho A\,Y_{n}\,Y_{m}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\ddot{\eta}_{n}
  + \sum_{n}
    \left(\int_{0}^{L} \rho A\,Y_{n}\,Y_{m}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\omega_{n}^{2}\,\eta_{n}
  = \int_{0}^{L} F(t)\,\delta(x-L)\,Y_{m}\,\mathrm{d}x.
\end{equation}
The orthogonality relation~\eqref{eq:orthogonality} eliminates all cross-terms
($n \neq m$), leaving one equation for each mode index $m$:
\begin{equation}
  \left(\int_{0}^{L} \rho A\,Y_{m}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\ddot{\eta}_{m}
  + \left(\int_{0}^{L} \rho A\,Y_{m}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\omega_{m}^{2}\,\eta_{m}
  = \int_{0}^{L} F(t)\,\delta(x-L)\,Y_{m}\,\mathrm{d}x.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Modal mass, stiffness and forcing.}
The \textbf{modal mass} is:
\begin{equation}
  m_{m} = \int_{0}^{L} \rho A\,Y_{m}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\rho A L}{2}.
  \label{eq:modal_mass}
\end{equation}
For a point force $F(t)$ applied at $x=L$, the Dirac delta collapses the
integral on the right-hand side:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{0}^{L} F(t)\,\delta(x - L)\,Y_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
  = F(t)\,Y_{m}(L),
\end{equation}
yielding the \textbf{modal forcing function} $F(t)\,Y_{m}(L)$ --- the force
modulated by the value of the mode shape at the forcing point.

The \textbf{decoupled modal equation} for mode $m$ is therefore:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
    m_{m}\,\ddot{\eta}_{m} + k_{m}\,\eta_{m} = F(t)\,Y_{m}(L)
  },
  \qquad
  k_{m} = m_{m}\,\omega_{m}^{2}
  \quad\text{(modal stiffness)}.
  \label{eq:modal_eq}
\end{equation}
This is the equation of a \textbf{simple harmonic oscillator} with mass $m_{m}$
and stiffness $k_{m}$, driven by a force proportional to $Y_{m}(L)$.  Due to
mode orthogonality, all equations~\eqref{eq:modal_eq} are \emph{completely
decoupled}: each $\eta_{m}(t)$ can be solved independently and the actual
physical response is then reconstructed as:
\begin{equation}
  \xi(x,t) = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} Y_{m}(x)\,\eta_{m}(t).
\end{equation}
In practice, only a \textbf{finite number of modes} can be retained in the
simulation, and the truncation level is chosen based on the highest frequency of
interest in the input.


\subsection{Bond-graph representation of the modal model}
\label{ssec:modal_bg}

To embed the modal model in a bond graph we introduce the \textbf{modal momentum}
and \textbf{modal displacement}:
\begin{equation}
  p_{m} = m_{m}\,\dot{\eta}_{m},
  \qquad
  q_{m} = \eta_{m}.
  \label{eq:modal_state_vars}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:modal_eq} then reads, in first-order form:
\begin{equation}
  \dot{p}_{m} = -k_{m}\,q_{m} + F(t)\,Y_{m}(L),
  \qquad
  \dot{q}_{m} = \frac{p_{m}}{m_{m}}.
  \label{eq:modal_state_eqs}
\end{equation}
These are exactly the state equations of an \textbf{I-element} ($I = m_{m}$) and
a \textbf{C-element} ($C = 1/k_{m}$) joined at a 1-junction, with the external
force connected through a \textbf{transformer} of modulus $Y_{m}(L)$.  The
velocity at the forcing point $x = L$ is recovered from the bond-graph structure
as:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\xi}{\partial t}(L,t)
  = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} Y_{m}(L)\,\dot{\eta}_{m}(t)
  = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} Y_{m}(L)\,\frac{p_{m}}{m_{m}},
\end{equation}
which corresponds to a 0-junction collecting the TF outputs.


\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/chapter7/7_4.png}
  \caption{Bond-graph for the $N$-mode bar}
\end{figure}

The construction above answers the question that naturally arises: \emph{why
build a bond graph when the PDE has already been ``solved'' analytically?}  The
answer is that the force $F(t)$ is in general \textbf{not} a specified input but
rather \emph{results from the interaction with some external sub-system}.  The
bond-graph modal model provides a \textbf{port} at $x = L$ with well-defined
effort and flow variables, so that any external subsystem (a mass--spring--damper,
an actuator, another flexible body) can be connected without further analysis.


\subsection{Reading outputs and applying forces at interior locations}
\label{ssec:interior_outputs}

A fundamental strength of the modal bond-graph approach is that
\textbf{any location} on the rod can be monitored as an output, and forces or
moments can be applied at arbitrary interior points, without any structural
modification to the modal model.

To read the velocity at an arbitrary interior location $x_{0}$, recall:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\xi}{\partial t}(x_{0},t)
  = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} Y_{m}(x_{0})\,\frac{p_{m}}{m_{m}}.
\end{equation}
In bond-graph terms this is a new 0-junction connected to the $m$ TF elements
with moduli $Y_{m}(x_{0})$, reading velocities from each modal I-element.

To \textbf{apply a force at an interior point} $x_{0}$, one introduces a new
0-junction at $x_{0}$ with its own $S_{E}$ source (or interface), and adds to
each mode $m$ an additional TF of modulus $Y_{m}(x_{0})$.  Alternatively, if
only output is desired (no power injected), the source is set to $S_{E}\colon 0$:
this defines the location without disturbing the dynamics.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/chapter7/7_5.png}
  \caption{Rod with three interface points}
\end{figure}

This ``cross-coupling'' of all modes to each interface point is visible in the
bond graph as a set of TF elements that fan out from each modal 1-junction to
all 0-junctions.  The structure resembles a web: each node is connected to
every other through the modal transformers.  While the visual complexity grows
with the number of modes and ports, the underlying mathematics remains fully
decoupled at the modal level --- the coupling only reappears at the physical
output level through the linear combination of mode shapes.

\begin{insightbox}[Exploiting the zero-force trick]
  Defining each output point as a zero-force input also allows one to
  \emph{act} on the beam at that location later, simply by replacing the
  zero source with a finite one.  This provides a clean and modular way to
  change the actuation architecture without rebuilding the modal model.
\end{insightbox}

\section{Application: heat transport in a bar}
\label{sec:heat}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{ssec:heat_problem}

As a second major application of the modal approach we consider the simulation
of the \textbf{temperature distribution} $T(t,x)$ in a bar of length $L$ moving
at a constant transport speed $\nu_{0}$ (material convection), with:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item External distributed heat sources $u_{i}(t)$ applied at
    discrete locations $x_{i}$ inside the bar.
  \item Radiation/convection from the bar surface to the ambient temperature
    $T_{0}$ with heat-transfer coefficient $h$.
  \item \textbf{Time-varying boundary conditions}: a prescribed temperature
    $T_{ST}(t)$ at $x=0$ (inlet) and an output heat flux $Q_{OUT}(t)$ at
    $x=L$ (outlet).
\end{itemize}

  \caption{Schematic of the heated moving bar}

\subsection{Derivation of the governing PDE}
\label{ssec:heat_pde}

The PDE is obtained via lumping over a small element $[x, x+\Delta x]$, exactly
as in the mechanical case, using the \textbf{pseudo-bond-graph framework}
introduced in the module on thermal systems.  The element is shown in the
following figure.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figs/chapter7/7_7.png}
  \caption{Thermal element between $x$ and $x+\Delta x$}
\end{figure}

For the \textbf{heat storage part}:
\begin{equation}
  \rho c\,\Delta x\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}(t,x)
  = Q(t,x+\Delta x) - Q(t,x) - Q_{d}(t,x),
  \label{eq:heat_storage}
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the bar density and $c$ its specific heat.  The \textbf{heat
flows} are:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta x\,Q(t,x) = k\bigl[T(t,x) - T(t,x-\Delta x)\bigr],
  \label{eq:heat_conduction}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  Q_{d}(t,x) = h\,\Delta x\bigl[T(t,x) - T_{0}\bigr],
  \label{eq:heat_radiation}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the thermal conductivity and $h$ is the surface heat-transfer
coefficient.

Starting from the simplified case $Q_{d}=0$ and letting $\Delta x \to 0$,
one recovers the classical \textbf{heat equation}:
\begin{equation}
  \rho c\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}(t,x)
  = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}(t,x),
  \qquad
  Q(t,x) = k\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}(t,x),
\end{equation}
which combined gives:
\begin{equation}
  \rho c\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}(t,x)
  = k\,\frac{\partial^{2}T}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)
  - h\bigl[T(t,x) - T_{0}\bigr].
  \label{eq:heat_eq}
\end{equation}

The full problem --- including heat transport at velocity $\nu_{0}$,
distributed point heat sources $u_{i}(t)\,\delta(x-x_{i})$, and radiation ---
is governed by:
\begin{equation}
  \rho c\,\frac{\partial\tilde{T}}{\partial t}(t,x)
  = \alpha\,\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{T}}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)
  - \nu_{0}\,\frac{\partial\tilde{T}}{\partial x}(t,x)
  - \beta\,\tilde{T}(t,x)
  + \sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta(x - x_{i})\,\tilde{u}_{i}(t),
  \label{eq:heat_full}
\end{equation}
with boundary conditions:
\begin{align}
  \tilde{T}(t,0) &= \tilde{T}_{ST}(t)
    \quad\text{(inlet temperature)},\\[4pt]
  \alpha\!\left[\frac{\partial\tilde{T}}{\partial x}(t,L)
    + \gamma_{2}\,\tilde{T}(t,L)\right]
    &= \tilde{Q}_{OUT}(t)
    \quad\text{(outlet flux)},
\end{align}
where $\alpha = k/(\rho c)$ is the thermal diffusivity, $\beta = h/(\rho c)$
accounts for surface radiation, and $\gamma_{2}$ is a parameter defined below.


\subsection{Coordinate transformation to remove transport and radiation}
\label{ssec:heat_transform}

A key step is to \textbf{``remove''} the first-order transport term and the
radiation term from the PDE, so that the remaining equation is in the standard
diffusion form to which separation of variables applies.  This is achieved by
the \textbf{exponential change of coordinates}:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{T}(t,x) = \mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{1}t + \gamma_{2}x}\,w(t,x),
  \label{eq:heat_transform}
\end{equation}
with the two parameters:
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{2} = \frac{\nu_{0}}{2\alpha},
  \qquad
  \gamma_{1} = -\beta - \nu_{0}\gamma_{2} + \alpha\gamma_{2}^{2}
  = -\beta - \frac{\nu_{0}^{2}}{4\alpha}.
  \label{eq:heat_params}
\end{equation}
Substituting \eqref{eq:heat_transform} into \eqref{eq:heat_full}, the transport
and radiation terms cancel and the new function $w(t,x)$ satisfies the
\textbf{standard diffusion equation} with source terms:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial w}{\partial t}(t,x)
  = \alpha\,\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)
  + \sum_{i=1}^{m}\delta(x - x_{i})\,v_{i}(t,x),
  \label{eq:heat_w_pde}
\end{equation}
with transformed inputs:
\begin{equation}
  v_{i}(t,x) = \mathrm{e}^{-(\gamma_{1}t + \gamma_{2}x)}\,\tilde{u}_{i}(t),
  \qquad i = 1,\ldots,m,
  \label{eq:heat_vi}
\end{equation}
and transformed boundary conditions:
\begin{align}
  w(t,0) &= W_{ST}(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-\gamma_{1}t}\,\tilde{T}_{ST}(t),\\[4pt]
  \alpha\!\left[\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}(t,L)
    + \gamma_{2}\,w(t,L)\right]
  &= \alpha\,H_{OUT}(t)
    = \mathrm{e}^{-(\gamma_{1}t + L\gamma_{2})}\,\tilde{Q}_{OUT}(t).
  \label{eq:heat_bc_transformed}
\end{align}
The transformation~\eqref{eq:heat_transform} is \emph{not} a change of
independent coordinates: $w$ and $\tilde{T}$ are evaluated at the same $(t,x)$.
It is rather a \textbf{dependent-variable scaling} that absorbs the exponential
spatial and temporal decay introduced by transport and radiation.


\subsection{Separation of variables for the heat equation}
\label{ssec:heat_sov}

We now apply separation of variables to~\eqref{eq:heat_w_pde} in two steps.

\paragraph{Step 1: handling time-varying boundary conditions.}

Time-varying boundary conditions prevent a direct application of the method.
Following a standard approach, we split $w$ as:
\begin{equation}
  z(t,x) = \bar{z}(t,x) + z_{0}(t) + x\,z_{L}(t),
  \label{eq:heat_split}
\end{equation}
where the lifting functions $z_{0}(t)$ and $x\,z_{L}(t)$ absorb the boundary
data, so that $\bar{z}$ satisfies \emph{homogeneous} boundary conditions:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{z}(t,0) = 0,
  \qquad
  \frac{\partial\bar{z}}{\partial x}(t,L) = 0.
\end{equation}
Note that this splitting is \textbf{not} a change of coordinates in the strict
sense: it is an additive decomposition that simplifies the boundary value
problem.

\paragraph{Step 2: modal expansion of the homogeneous problem.}

The homogeneous problem for $\bar{z}$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial\bar{z}}{\partial t}(t,x)
  = \alpha\,\frac{\partial^{2}\bar{z}}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x),
  \qquad
  \bar{z}(t,0) = 0,\quad
  \frac{\partial\bar{z}}{\partial x}(t,L) = 0,
  \label{eq:heat_homog}
\end{equation}
has the same boundary conditions as the unforced bar (zero temperature at
$x=0$, zero flux at $x=L$).  The \textbf{mode shapes} are therefore:
\begin{equation}
  Z_{n}(x) = \sin\!\left[(2n+1)\,\frac{\pi}{2}\,\frac{x}{L}\right],
  \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots,
  \label{eq:heat_modes}
\end{equation}
and the solution takes the form:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{z}(t,x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} Z_{n}(x)\,f_{n}(t).
  \label{eq:heat_expansion}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Final modal equations for the heat problem}
\label{ssec:heat_modal_eqs}

After substituting the expansion~\eqref{eq:heat_expansion} into
the full PDE (including the boundary-lifting terms, the transformed sources
$v_{i}$, and the boundary output condition), and exploiting the orthogonality
of the mode shapes, one arrives at the \textbf{coupled modal ODE system}:
\begin{align}
  w(t,x) &= \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} Z_{n}(x)\,\eta'_{n}(t),
  \label{eq:heat_w_expansion}\\[6pt]
  w_{L}(t)
  &= -\gamma_{2}\,w(t,L) + H_{OUT}(t)
   = -\frac{\nu_{0}}{2\alpha}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} Z_{n}(L)\,\eta'_{n}(t)
     + H_{OUT}(t) \notag\\
  &= -\frac{\nu_{0}}{2\alpha}\sum_{\bar{n}=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{\bar{n}}\,\eta'_{\bar{n}}(t)
     + H_{OUT}(t),
  \label{eq:heat_wL}
\end{align}
and the evolution equation for each modal amplitude:
\begin{align}
  \dot{\eta}'_{n}(t)
  &= -\frac{(2n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}\alpha}{4L^{2}}\,\eta'_{n}(t)
     - \frac{\nu_{0}}{L}\sum_{\bar{n}=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{(n+\bar{n})}\,\eta'_{\bar{n}}(t)
     + \frac{2}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{m} Z_{n}(x_{i})\,v_{i}(t,x_{i})
     \notag\\
  &\quad
     + \frac{(2n+1)\pi\alpha}{L^{2}}\,W_{ST}(t)
     + (-1)^{n}\,\frac{2\alpha}{L}\,H_{OUT}(t).
  \label{eq:heat_modal_eqs}
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:heat_modal_eqs} is the central result of the heat-problem
analysis.  Let us examine its structure term by term:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=4pt]
  \item The first term, $-(2n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}\alpha/(4L^{2})\;\eta'_{n}$, is the
    standard \textbf{modal decay} of the $n$-th diffusion mode: it comes from the
    spatial second derivative and gives the mode a characteristic relaxation time
    $\tau_{n} = 4L^{2}/[(2n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}\alpha]$.  Higher modes decay faster.
  \item The second term, $-(\nu_{0}/L)\sum_{\bar{n}}(-1)^{(n+\bar{n})}\eta'_{\bar{n}}$,
    arises from the outlet flux boundary condition which, after the coordinate
    change, introduces a \textbf{coupling between all modes}.  This is the
    price paid for non-zero transport: the modes are no longer fully decoupled.
  \item The third term, $\frac{2}{L}\sum_{i} Z_{n}(x_{i})\,v_{i}(t,x_{i})$,
    is the \textbf{modal forcing} from the distributed point-wise heat sources.
    The mode-shape value $Z_{n}(x_{i})$ plays the same role as $Y_{n}(L)$ in
    the mechanical bar.
  \item The fourth term involves $W_{ST}(t)$: the \textbf{inlet temperature
    boundary condition} drives each mode with a gain proportional to $(2n+1)$.
  \item The fifth term involves $H_{OUT}(t)$: the \textbf{outlet flux} drives
    mode $n$ with an alternating sign $(-1)^{n}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{insightbox}[Coupling introduced by transport]
  In the purely diffusive case ($\nu_{0} = 0$, i.e.\ $\gamma_{2}=0$), the
  boundary condition at $x=L$ becomes simply $\partial w/\partial x = 0$
  (zero flux), and the second term in \eqref{eq:heat_modal_eqs} disappears.
  The modal equations become fully decoupled --- each mode evolves
  independently, as in the mechanical bar.  Transport introduces
  inter-modal coupling: physically, the advection of heat modifies the
  effective boundary condition in a way that mixes modes.  The coupling
  is weighted by $(-1)^{(n+\bar{n})}$, so adjacent modes interact most
  strongly with alternating signs.
\end{insightbox}

\begin{warningbox}[Linearity is required]
  The entire separation-of-variables and modal-decomposition machinery
  applies \textbf{only to linear PDEs}.  The coordinate transformation
  \eqref{eq:heat_transform} is linear; the modal expansion
  \eqref{eq:heat_expansion} is linear; and the decoupling by orthogonality
  is linear.  If the heat-transfer coefficient $h$ depends on temperature,
  or if the transport velocity $\nu_{0}$ is state-dependent, the approach
  breaks down and one must resort to finite-difference, finite-element, or
  other nonlinear numerical schemes.
\end{warningbox}


%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Summary}
\label{sec:distrib_summary}
%──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Two complementary approaches for modelling distributed-parameter systems have
been presented.  Their salient properties are summarised below.

\paragraph{Simple (finite) lumping.}
A lumped-parameter model can be found for a distributed system by describing the
behaviour of a small element; however, such a model may not be accurate without
retaining a large number of small elements.  The lumped-parameter representations
have the advantage that they can be \emph{easily combined} into an overall system
model and non-linear effects pose no particular problems.  However, the model has
a \textbf{large state space} and can cause computational problems due to
\emph{disparities in the time scales} of the distributed portion of the model and
the remainder of the system.

\paragraph{Continuum (PDE) models.}
Continuum models can yield insight into system behaviour when analytical solutions
are available.  However, it is difficult to incorporate a continuum model into an
overall system model, mostly because of the difficulties in solving the PDEs for
the continuum part subject to complicated boundary conditions, and the inability
to handle non-linear effects analytically.

\paragraph{Modal (separation-of-variables) models.}
The modal approach bridges the two: it takes the PDE as its starting point (full
accuracy of the continuum), applies separation of variables to decouple the
spatial and temporal dependences, and produces a \textbf{finite-order
lumped-parameter bond graph} that retains spectral accuracy up to the number of
modes included.  The resulting model:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item Has all storage elements in \emph{integral causality}.
  \item Can be seamlessly coupled to bond-graph models of the rest of the system.
  \item Allows forces, moments, or heat sources to be applied (and outputs to be
    read) at arbitrary locations by evaluating the mode shapes at those points.
  \item Admits the addition of modal damping (R-elements on each modal junction).
  \item Applies to \emph{general linear PDEs} --- wave equation, heat equation,
    beam equation --- not only to wave-like systems.
\end{enumerate}
The trade-off is that \textbf{linearity is mandatory}: non-linear distributed
effects force a return to finite lumping or full numerical PDE solvers.
