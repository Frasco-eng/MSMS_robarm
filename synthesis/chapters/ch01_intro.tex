\section{The systems viewpoint}

A \textbf{system} is any entity that can be conceptually separated from its environment by a physical or conceptual boundary.
Inside the boundary are interacting subsystems and components.

\begin{insightbox}[The modelling trade-off]
No system can be modelled exactly. The art is to choose the \textbf{right level of complexity}.
A model that is too simple will miss important dynamics;
one that is too detailed will obscure the physics and be computationally intractable.
This judgement is acquired through practice and physical intuition.
\end{insightbox}

Dynamic models serve four distinct purposes: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Analysis}: Predict the future given known inputs and initial conditions. This can be data-based (black box) or physics-law based (white box) depending on how the component is characterized.
    \item \textbf{Identification}: Find the model from measured input/output histories.
    \item \textbf{Synthesis}: Find the system that turns a given input into a desired output.
    \item \textbf{Control}: Design a feedback law that drives the system to a desired behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\section{Power as the universal currency}

The central idea of bond graph theory is that \textbf{all physical interactions involve the exchange of power}.
When a motor drives a pump, when a spring pushes a mass, when a voltage source drives a current through a resistor, in every case, energy flows across a boundary, and the instantaneous rate of that flow is power.
By making power the primary language, bond graphs work equally well in every energy domain.
At every interconnection point between subsystems, called a \textbf{port}, two variables are defined whose product is instantaneous power:
\begin{equation}
  P(t) = e(t)\,f(t)
\end{equation}
These are called the \textbf{effort} $e$ and the \textbf{flow} $f$.
Their specific physical meaning depends on the domain:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lllll}
  \toprule
  \textbf{Domain} & \textbf{Effort} $e$ & \textbf{Flow} $f$ & \textbf{Power} $ef$ \\
  \midrule
  Mechanical (translation) & Force $F$ [N] & Velocity $V$ [m/s] & [W] \\
  Mechanical (rotation)    & Torque $\tau$ [N$\cdot$m] & Ang.\ velocity $\omega$ [rad/s] & [W] \\
  Electrical               & Voltage $e$ [V] & Current $i$ [A] & [W] \\
  Hydraulic                & Pressure $P$ [Pa] & Volume flow $Q$ [m$^3$/s] & [W] \\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Two additional variables complete the picture.
The \textbf{momentum} $p$ (generalised) is the time integral of effort;
the \textbf{displacement} $q$ is the time integral of flow:
\begin{equation}
  p(t) = p_0 + \int_{t_0}^t e(\tau)\,d\tau,
  \qquad
  q(t) = q_0 + \int_{t_0}^t f(\tau)\,d\tau,
  \qquad
  \dot{p} = e,\quad \dot{q} = f.
\end{equation}
These are the \textbf{energy variables} (or state variables). 
While power is the instantaneous flow, energy flow is the time integral of that power:

$$
E(t) = \int^t P(\tau) d\tau = \int^t e(\tau)f(\tau) d\tau
$$

In cases where an effort is a function of displacement, the energy is $$ E(q) = \int e(q) dq $$

Conversely, when flow is a function of momentum, $$E(p) = \int f(p) dp$$.

The relationship between these four variables is elegantly summarized by the \textbf{Tetrahedron of State}:

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth, node distance=2.5cm]
  \node (e) at (0, 2) {$e$};
  \node (f) at (0, -2) {$f$};
  \node (p) at (-2, 0) {$p$};
  \node (q) at (2, 0) {$q$};

  \draw[->, thick] (e) to[bend right] node[left] {$\int dt$} (p);
  \draw[->, thick] (f) to[bend right] node[right] {$\int dt$} (q);
  \draw[->, thick] (p) to[bend right] node[right] {$\frac{d}{dt}$} (e);
  \draw[->, thick] (q) to[bend right] node[left] {$\frac{d}{dt}$} (f);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}


\section{Bonds, ports and multiports}

A \textbf{subsystem with one or more ports} is called a \textbf{multiport}.
Each port is a place where power can flow in or out.
Familiar examples: an electric motor is a 3-port (field winding, armature winding, mechanical shaft);
a hydraulic pump is a 2-port (shaft, fluid line); a mechanical spring is a 1-port.
When two multiports are connected, meaning one port of each is joined, power flows between them and the effort and flow at the shared port become identical for both.
This shared port is a \textbf{bond}, drawn as a line with a half-arrow indicating the assumed positive direction of power flow:

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node[draw,rounded corners=3pt,fill=gray!10,minimum width=2cm,minimum height=1cm]
    (A) at (0,0) {System A};
  \node[draw,rounded corners=3pt,fill=gray!10,minimum width=2cm,minimum height=1cm]
    (B) at (5,0) {System B};
  \draw[thick] (A.east) -- node[above]{$e$} node[below]{$f$} (B.west);
  \draw[->,thick] (2.0,0) -- (2.8,0);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

When major sub-systems are represented by words instead of internal details, the resulting graph is known as a \textbf{word bond graph}. 
The half-arrow is \emph{not} the direction of causality and \emph{not necessarily} the direction energy actually flows at any given instant, it simply sets the sign convention: power is positive when it flows in the direction of the arrow.

\begin{defbox}[Bond]
A bond is a line connecting two ports. It carries \emph{both} an effort $e$ and a flow $f$ simultaneously.
The product $ef$ is the instantaneous power flowing in the direction of the half-arrow.
A bond is a 2-wire connection in the metaphorical sense: it always transmits a pair of conjugate variables.
\end{defbox}

\section{Causality: inputs, outputs and causal strokes}

At any port, one of the two power variables is the \textbf{input} (cause) and the other is the \textbf{output} (effect).
One can either impose a force and observe the resulting velocity, or impose a velocity and observe the resulting force, but \textbf{never both simultaneously}.
This choice is called the \textbf{causality} of the port.

Causality is indicated on a bond graph by a \textbf{causal stroke}: a short perpendicular bar at one end of the bond.
It is critical to remember that the half-arrow power direction indications and the causal stroke are \textbf{totally uncorrelated}.
The rule is:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=2pt]
  \item The causal stroke marks the end toward which the \textbf{effort} is being directed (effort is an input to that end).
  \item The \textbf{flow} signal is then directed \emph{away} from the stroked end (flow is an output of that end).
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node (A) at (0,0) {A};
  \node (B) at (4,0) {B};
  \draw[thick] (A.east) -- (B.west);
  \draw[thick] (3.8,-0.2) -- (3.8,0.2); % causal stroke at B end
  \node[font=\footnotesize] at (2,0.3) {$e$: input to B, output of A};
  \node[font=\footnotesize] at (2,-0.3) {$f$: output of B, input to A};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Causal assignment is not free, each type of element has constraints on which causalities are admissible.
The choice of causality throughout the bond graph propagates and constrains subsequent choices, and ultimately determines whether the system equations are explicit ODEs, implicit DAEs, or contain algebraic loops.
This is detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:causality}.

\section{Pure signal flow and active bonds}

Not all interconnections transmit significant power.
A sensor measuring a velocity transmits negligible power back to the mechanism it measures;
an electronic amplifier takes a tiny input signal and produces a much larger output, with power supplied by an external source.
In these cases, one of the two power variables is negligible, and the bond \textbf{degenerates to a single signal}, called an \textbf{active bond}, drawn with a full arrow rather than a half-arrow.
Because their description is functional rather than physical, ideal instruments and amplifiers represented by these bonds violate many physical laws. 
Active bonds are used to represent signal processing, controllers, and ideal sensors within an otherwise power-based bond graph.
The combination of bond graphs (for the physical plant) and block diagrams (for the controller) is the natural language for mechatronic system design.
